# Abandoned but Not Forgotten — Story Storyboard

Nov 14, 2026 · @Lawrence

The story runs as an opening scene plus 13 chapters, one per spacecraft, in the order each one reached the place it still sits today. A child unlocks the next chapter each time they pick up a piece of litter.

## How to use this storyboard

Every chapter card uses the same five parts, so the app can load them the same way:

1. **Where it is now** and the date it got there.
2. **What it was** in one line a 9-year-old can say back.
3. **What it did**: two or three discoveries.
4. **Why it was left there.**
5. **Still helping us**: what we still get from it today, or what it taught later missions.

Each card also has a **kid hook**: one surprising fact to read aloud, and a **pick-up line**: what the narrator says when the next piece of litter is collected.

## Opening scene: 96 bags on the Moon

Before any litter is picked up, the user presses to 'Begin' the journey and the narrator explains that astronauts left over 90 days of human waste on the Moon. That leads into the game: we clean up our trash, and we visit the things people left in space on purpose.

**Narrator script (draft):**

> *Between 1969 and 1972, twelve astronauts walked on the Moon. To fly home, their spaceship had to be as light as possible, and they wanted room for Moon rocks. So they threw things out: over 90 bags of poop, pee and sick! They are still sitting on the Moon today.*
>
> *But not everything left in space is rubbish. Some of the machines we left behind are still helping scientists right now. Each time you pick up a piece of litter, you unlock one of those machines.*

**Kid hook:** Scientists would love to go back and check those bags. They want to know if any germs could survive 50 years on the Moon!

**Pick-up line:** "The astronauts couldn't take their trash home, but you can take care of ours. Find your first piece of litter to unlock the first spacecraft!"

**Safety line (shown before the first scan):** "Put on gloves and bring a grown-up with you. Never touch glass, needles or anything sharp. If you see one, tell a grown-up."

## Chapter 1: Surveyor 3, the robot that tested the ground (Moon, 1967)

**Where it is now:** The Ocean of Storms on the Moon. (It isn't a real ocean. It's a huge, flat, dusty plain!) It landed on 20 April 1967.

**What it was:** A robot with a TV camera and a little scoop on an arm. Its job was to check if the Moon's ground was safe for astronauts to land on.

**What it did:**

- Sent more than 6,000 photos back to Earth.
- Dug four little trenches with its scoop, a bit like digging at the beach.
- Found that Moon soil is a lot like wet sand, and strong enough to hold up a spaceship with astronauts inside.

**Why it was left there:** It was never meant to come home. When the long, cold Moon night began, it went quiet. The last message from it came on 4 May 1967.

**Still helping us:** Two and a half years later, astronauts landed right next to it! They took its scoop and camera back to Earth, so scientists could see what the Moon does to a machine.

**Kid hook:** Surveyor 3 didn't land on its first try. It bounced! Its engine stayed on too long, so it hopped twice, once as high as a three-storey building.

**Pick-up line:** "Surveyor 3 checked the ground was safe. Pick up another piece to meet the first astronauts on the Moon!"

Source: [NASA Science, Surveyor 3](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/surveyor-3/)

## Chapter 2: Apollo 11 Eagle and the Moon mirror (Moon, 1969)

**Where it is now:** The Sea of Tranquility on the Moon. The astronauts called their landing spot Tranquility Base. They landed on 20 July 1969.

**What it was:** Eagle, the first spaceship to land people on the Moon. Inside were Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin.

**What was left behind:** Eagle's bottom half with a sign (a plaque) on it, a flag, a machine that could feel moonquakes, and a special mirror.

**What it did:**

- Neil Armstrong became the first person ever to step onto the Moon.
- The astronauts brought home about 22 kg of Moon rocks and dust, about as heavy as a 7-year-old.
- Their moonquake detector listened to the Moon for more than a month.

**Why it was left there:** Leaving the heavy bottom half behind made the top half light enough to fly the astronauts back up. The plaque on it says: "We came in peace for all mankind."

**Still helping us:** Scientists on Earth still shine lasers at the mirror and time how long the light takes to bounce back. That tells them how far away the Moon is, to less than 1 cm!

**Kid hook:** The mirror showed that the Moon is slowly drifting away from Earth, about 4 cm every year. That's about how fast your fingernails grow!

**Pick-up line:** "Eagle left a mirror we still use today! Pick up another piece to see who visited Surveyor 3."

Sources: [NASA NSSDCA, Apollo 11 Lunar Module/EASEP](https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/nmc/spacecraft/display.action?id=1969-059C); laser ranging figures from [Apollo 11 and Fundamental Science (arXiv)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2005.09332) and [IEEE Spectrum](https://spectrum.ieee.org/amp/one-apollo-11-experiment-is-still-going-50-years-later-2650278911), not from a NASA page.

## Chapter 3: Apollo 12 Intrepid, the visit to an old robot (Moon, 1969)

**Where it is now:** The Ocean of Storms on the Moon, just a short walk (180 m) from Surveyor 3. It landed on 19 November 1969.

**What it was:** Intrepid, the second spaceship to land people on the Moon. Inside were Pete Conrad and Alan Bean.

**What was left behind:** Intrepid's bottom half and a science station. The station had tools to feel moonquakes, measure magnetism, and catch the "solar wind" (tiny bits that stream out from the Sun). A small nuclear battery kept it running.

**What it did:**

- The astronauts walked over to Surveyor 3 and took some of its parts home.
- They collected about 34 kg of Moon rocks and soil.
- Their science station kept sending messages to Earth for almost eight years, until 1977.

**Why it was left there:** The science station was meant to stay and keep listening after the astronauts flew home.

**Still helping us:** After the astronauts left, NASA crashed Intrepid's empty top half into the Moon on purpose. The science station felt it: the first moonquake ever made by people!

**Kid hook:** The astronauts' TV camera broke on the very first day. Someone pointed it at the Sun by mistake!

**Pick-up line:** "Intrepid made the Moon shake! Pick up another piece to meet the Moon's first car."

Source: [NASA NSSDCA, Apollo 12 Lunar Module/ALSEP](https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/nmc/spacecraft/display.action?id=1969-099C)

## Chapter 4: Apollo 15 Falcon and the first Moon car (Moon, 1971)

**Where it is now:** Hadley–Apennine on the Moon, at the foot of a big mountain range. It landed on 30 July 1971.

**What it was:** Falcon, a spaceship carrying astronauts Dave Scott and Jim Irwin. It brought something brand new: a car for driving on the Moon, called the Lunar Roving Vehicle.

**What was left behind:** Falcon's bottom half, the Moon car, a science station, a mirror for lasers, and a little figure and sign to remember 14 American and Soviet space explorers who had died.

**What it did:**

- The astronauts drove the Moon car almost 28 km, much farther than earlier crews could walk.
- They brought home about 77 kg of rocks and soil, plus a tube of soil dug up from 3 m underground.
- A heat probe in the ground measured how much heat escapes from deep inside the Moon.

**Why it was left there:** Falcon's bottom half was the launch pad for its top half, so it had to stay behind. The Moon car stayed too.

**Still helping us:** The crew left a laser mirror that is even bigger than Apollo 11's. Scientists still bounce lasers off it today.

**Kid hook:** Dave Scott dropped a hammer and a feather at the same time, on live TV. There's no air on the Moon to slow the feather down, so they hit the ground together!

**Pick-up line:** "The Moon car is still parked up there! Pick up another piece and blast off into deep space."

Sources: [NASA NSSDCA, Apollo 15 Lunar Module/ALSEP](https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/nmc/spacecraft/display.action?id=1971-063C); mirror size from [Apollo 11 and Fundamental Science (arXiv)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2005.09332).

## Chapter 5: Pioneer 10, the message in a bottle (deep space, 1972)

**Where it is now:** Flying out of our solar system, heading toward a red star called Aldebaran. It was launched on 2 March 1972.

**What it was:** The first spacecraft NASA sent out to the giant planets. It was built to last 21 months, but it kept going for more than 30 years!

**What it did:**

- It was the first spacecraft to fly through the asteroid belt, a huge ring of space rocks between Mars and Jupiter.
- It was the first to fly past Jupiter, in 1973. It took about 500 pictures, including Jupiter's giant storm, the Great Red Spot.
- In 1983 it became the first human-made object to travel past Neptune.

**Why it was left there:** Its nuclear power source slowly ran down. Its last message reached us in 2003, from 12 billion km away. That message took more than 11 hours to get to Earth!

**Still helping us:** Pioneer 10 was hit by fewer space rocks than scientists feared. That proved spacecraft can cross the asteroid belt safely. It also carries a metal sign that tells anyone who finds it where it came from.

**Kid hook:** Pioneer 10 should pass the star Aldebaran in about two million years.

**Pick-up line:** "Pioneer 10 is still sailing with its message. Pick up another piece and land on Mars!"

Source: [NASA Science, Pioneer 10](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/pioneer-10/)

## Chapter 6: Viking 1, the first to land safely on Mars (Mars, 1976)

**Where it is now:** Chryse Planitia (the Golden Plain) on Mars. It landed on 20 July 1976, seven years to the day after Apollo 11 landed on the Moon.

**What it was:** A robot lander with an arm and a tiny science lab, sent to look for signs of life on Mars. A partner spacecraft flew in circles around Mars above it.

**What it did:**

- Made the first truly successful landing on Mars and took colour photos of its surroundings.
- Scooped up Mars soil and tested it for life in its lab. The results were puzzling, and it found none of the carbon chemicals that living things are made of.
- Measured the temperature: −86 °C before sunrise and −33 °C in the afternoon.
- The two Viking spacecraft circling Mars mapped almost all of it (97%).

**Why it was left there:** It sent weather reports from Mars for six years. Then, in November 1982, a wrong command sent from Earth cut it off, and we never heard from it again.

**Still helping us:** Viking gave us our first close-up look at Mars, and showed how tricky it is to search for life on another planet. The lander is now named the Thomas Mutch Memorial Station, after the leader of its camera team.

**Kid hook:** Even on a sunny afternoon, Viking measured −33 °C. That's colder than the inside of a freezer!

**Pick-up line:** "Viking sent weather reports from Mars for six years! Pick up another piece to meet the first Mars rover."

Source: [NASA Science, Viking 1](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/viking-1/)

## Chapter 7: Mars Pathfinder and Sojourner, the bouncing beach ball (Mars, 1997)

**Where it is now:** Ares Vallis on Mars, a valley where a giant flood once rushed through. It landed on 4 July 1997.

**What it was:** A lander and Sojourner, the first rover (robot car) on Mars. Sojourner was tiny: about the size of a microwave oven, and just 30 cm tall.

**What it did:**

- Took more than 16,500 photos from the lander and 550 from the rover.
- Found round pebbles which suggest that, long ago, Mars was warmer and had running water.
- Found that Mars dust is magnetic, and measured spinning dust devils.

**Why it was left there:** It kept working until its last message on 27 September 1997. Then it went quiet.

**Still helping us:** Pathfinder tried a brand-new way to land: wrapped up in giant airbags! It bounced about 15 times, up to 15 m high, before rolling to a stop. Spirit and Opportunity later landed with airbags too.

**Kid hook:** Scientists think the flood that made this valley held as much water as North America's Great Lakes, and it all rushed through in about two weeks!

**Pick-up line:** "Boing, boing, boing! Pick up another piece to visit a spacecraft that landed on an asteroid."

Source: [NASA Science, Mars Pathfinder](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/mars-pathfinder/); size comparison from [NASA Space Place](https://spaceplace.nasa.gov/mars-sojourner)

## Chapter 8: NEAR Shoemaker, the surprise asteroid landing (asteroid Eros, 2001)

**Where it is now:** Sitting on Eros, an asteroid (a big space rock) about 34 km long. It landed on 12 February 2001.

**What it was:** NEAR, a spacecraft built to fly around an asteroid, not to land on one. It was later renamed NEAR Shoemaker, after the scientist Eugene Shoemaker.

**What it did:**

- It was the first spacecraft ever to orbit (fly in circles around) an asteroid.
- It took 160,000 pictures, about 10 times more than planned.
- It found that Eros has no magnetic field, and mapped most of its surface.

**Why it was left there:** At the end of its mission, the team lowered it gently onto the asteroid, at about walking speed. It wasn't built to land, but it survived! It kept sending messages for about two weeks, until the freezing cold stopped it.

**Still helping us:** NEAR was the first spacecraft to orbit an asteroid and the first to land on one. It showed we can get up close to a space rock and study it.

**Kid hook:** NEAR started circling Eros on Valentine's Day 2000. Eros is named after the Greek god of love!

**Pick-up line:** "First to land on an asteroid! Pick up another piece and meet the twin Mars rovers."

Source: [NASA Science, NEAR Shoemaker](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/near-shoemaker/)

## Chapter 9: Spirit and Opportunity, the twin robot geologists (Mars, 2004)

**Where they are now:** On opposite sides of Mars. Spirit landed in Gusev Crater on 3 January 2004. Opportunity landed at Meridiani Planum three weeks later, on 24 January.

**What they were:** Twin robot geologists (rock scientists) powered by sunlight. Their job was to find out if Mars once had water.

**What they did:**

- They studied rocks and proved that, a very long time ago, water stayed on Mars for ages.
- Spirit drove almost 8 km.
- Opportunity drove about 45 km, farther than a marathon! For more than 20 years, nothing had driven farther on another world.

**Why they were left there:** Spirit finished its mission in 2010. In June 2018, a giant dust storm covered the whole of Mars and blocked the sunlight Opportunity needed for power. We never heard from it again.

**Still helping us:** What the twins found out about water is a big part of what we know about Mars's past. Opportunity's driving record lasted until October 2026, when the newer rover Perseverance finally beat it.

**Kid hook:** Sometimes the Martian wind blew the dust off Opportunity's solar panels, like a free car wash, so it could soak up more sunlight.

**Pick-up line:** "The twins showed Mars was once wet! Pick up another piece and go planet-hunting."

Source: [NASA Science, Spirit and Opportunity](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/mars-exploration-rovers-spirit-and-opportunity/)

## Chapter 10: Kepler, the planet hunter (orbiting the Sun, 2009)

**Where it is now:** Circling the Sun on its own, safely away from Earth. It was launched on 6 March 2009.

**What it was:** A space telescope that hunted for planets around other stars. When a planet passes in front of its star, the star looks a tiny bit dimmer. Kepler watched for those tiny dips.

**What it did:**

- It found more than 2,600 planets around other stars.
- It found the first Earth-size planets at just the right distance from their stars for water to stay liquid.
- It found that the most common kind of planet is one our solar system doesn't have: bigger than Earth but smaller than Neptune.

**Why it was left there:** In 2013, two of the wheels it used for steering broke. Engineers had a clever idea: they used the gentle push of sunlight to keep it steady, "like a kayak steering into the current". It finally ran out of fuel in 2018.

**Still helping us:** Kepler's data is free for anyone to use, and scientists still study it. It showed that 20–50% of the stars you can see at night probably have small, rocky planets where water could exist.

**Kid hook:** When the mission was first imagined, nobody knew of a single planet outside our solar system. Now we know planets are everywhere.

**Pick-up line:** "Planets everywhere! Pick up another piece and fly to the biggest thing in the asteroid belt."

Source: [NASA Science, Kepler](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/kepler/)

## Chapter 11: Dawn, the spaceship with the gentle engine (orbiting Ceres, 2015)

**Where it is now:** Circling Ceres, a dwarf planet in the asteroid belt between Mars and Jupiter. It arrived on 5 March 2015.

**What it was:** A spaceship with a very gentle engine. It pushed only about as hard as a sheet of paper presses on your hand, but it could keep pushing for years. That let Dawn visit two worlds: the giant asteroid Vesta, and then Ceres.

**What it did:**

- It mapped Ceres, which is about 940 km wide, swooping as low as 35 km above the ground.
- It found clues that Ceres may still be changing today, with salty water that stays liquid even in the cold.
- It found ammonia, a clue that Ceres may have formed far out in the solar system and moved closer.

**Why it was left there:** In 2018 it ran out of the fuel it needed to point its solar panels at the Sun and its antenna at Earth.

**Still helping us:** Dawn's discoveries help scientists understand icy worlds, like the frozen moons of the giant planets. Dawn will keep circling Ceres for decades, like a monument in space.

**Kid hook:** Ceres is about one quarter ice. Apart from Earth, nothing in the inner solar system holds more water!

**Pick-up line:** "Dawn is still circling Ceres! Pick up another piece and go back to Mars to listen for marsquakes."

Sources: [NASA Science, Dawn](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/dawn/), [Ceres science page](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/dawn/science/ceres/), [Mission status](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/dawn/mission-status/), [FAQ](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/dawn/faq/)

## Chapter 12: InSight, the lander that took Mars's pulse (Mars, 2018)

**Where it is now:** Elysium Planitia, a flat, smooth plain near the middle of Mars. It landed on 26 November 2018.

**What it was:** A lander that studied the inside of Mars, a bit like a doctor checking your pulse and temperature. Its long robot arm placed a quake detector and a heat probe on the ground.

**What it did:**

- It felt the first marsquakes ever recorded. The biggest one made Mars shake for at least six hours!
- It found that the centre of Mars is a ball of liquid metal, bigger than scientists expected.
- It felt a big space rock crash into Mars in 2021. Spacecraft then found the crash had dug up hidden ice.
- It noticed thousands of dust devils (spinning winds) passing by.

**Why it was left there:** Its last contact was on 15 December 2022, as its power ran out.

**Still helping us:** That hidden ice could one day give astronauts water to drink, help them grow food, and even make rocket fuel. Spacecraft circling Mars still take photos of InSight to see how dust moves.

**Kid hook:** InSight could "feel" dust devils go past. They tugged on the ground like a giant vacuum cleaner!

**Pick-up line:** "Mars has quakes! Pick up your last piece to unlock the first helicopter on another planet."

Sources: [NASA Science, InSight](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/insight/), [InSight science highlights](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/insight/science-highlights/)

## Chapter 13: Ingenuity, the first helicopter on another planet (Mars, 2021)

**Where it is now:** Jezero Crater on Mars. It arrived on 18 February 2021, tucked under the belly of the Perseverance rover.

**What it was:** A small helicopter. It was only meant to try up to five test flights.

**What it did:**

- On 19 April 2021, it made the first powered flight on another planet.
- It flew 72 times in nearly three years, travelled 17 km, and climbed as high as 24 m.
- It scouted from the sky to help the Perseverance team decide where to go.

**Why it was left there:** It made its last flight on 18 January 2024, and its mission ended a week later.

**Still helping us:** Ingenuity proved helicopters can fly in Mars's super-thin air. NASA is now planning SkyFall, a team of new Mars helicopters that will look for landing spots for astronauts and search for ice underground.

**Kid hook:** Ingenuity was supposed to fly 5 times. It flew 72!

**Pick-up line:** "You've collected every piece! Time for the finale."

Source: [NASA Science, Ingenuity Mars Helicopter](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/mars-2020-perseverance/ingenuity-mars-helicopter/)

## Finale

**Narrator script (draft):**

> *You picked up 13 pieces of litter and visited 13 machines that people left in space. Some are still helping us, like the mirrors on the Moon that tell us exactly how far away it is. Others taught us how to build the next explorers.*
>
> *They were left behind, but they're not forgotten. Nobody can tidy up the Moon or Mars yet. Here on Earth, you just did!*

**Ending screen:** the child's count of pieces collected, a badge for each chapter unlocked, and a map that shows all 13 resting places.
